Analysis

AI Companionship & Consumer Aversion · Step 4 of 5

From experimental comparisons to psychological mechanisms: what happens when consumers observe others forming intimate relationships with AI?

The analytical story

Across three experiments, exposure to norm-violating human–AI interactions increased privacy concern and reduced willingness to share personal data. Reduced AI-related perceived control helped explain these responses, particularly when AI was framed as having low autonomy.

01 From Data to Evidence

The analysis addresses three connected questions:

Observe — Does AI companionship trigger aversion?

Compare privacy concern and willingness to share personal data between participants exposed to conventional AI use and those exposed to romantic human–AI relationships.

Explain — Why might observers become averse to AI?

Examine whether observing norm-violating human–AI interactions reduces AI-related perceived control, and whether this reduction statistically accounts for part of the observed AI aversion.

Qualify — When is the psychological pathway stronger?

Test whether perceived AI autonomy changes the indirect relationship between observed norm violation and consumer aversion through perceived control.

02 Experimental Evidence: Observing AI Intimacy Increases Aversion

Study 1 — Interpersonal Exposure

N = 198 · Randomised between-subjects experiment

Participants imagined a friend introducing either an AI work assistant or an AI romantic partner.

The analysis first established whether the scenarios were perceived differently.

Manipulation Checks

Participants exposed to the romantic AI relationship reported:

  • Higher perceived social norm violation (5.73 vs. 3.89; p < .001).
  • Lower schema congruity (3.39 vs. 4.72; p < .001).

These results confirmed that the two scenarios conveyed different expectations about AI's social role.

What the Three Experiments Establish

The direction of the effects was consistent across interpersonal communication, advertising, and news scenarios.

Observing human–AI interaction that was high in norm violation led to higher reported privacy concern and lower willingness to share personal data.

Key finding: Consumer aversion to AI can arise from observing other people's interactions with AI, without direct use of the technology.

03 Mechanism: The Role of Perceived Control

Why Does Observing AI Intimacy Increase Aversion?

Study 2 investigated whether AI-related perceived control could help explain the relationship between observed norm violation and AI aversion.

The proposed psychological process was grounded in Compensatory Control Theory.

When human–AI relationships appear inconsistent with established expectations about AI's appropriate role, observers may perceive AI as less predictable and manageable.

Reduced AI-related perceived control may then be associated with stronger privacy concern and lower willingness to share personal data.

Step 1 — Test the Effect on Perceived Control

Participants exposed to the AI romantic companion advertisement reported significantly lower perceived control than participants exposed to the conventional AI assistant advertisement.

Separate mediation analyses were conducted using PROCESS Model 4 with 5,000 bootstrap samples.

Path A — Observed Norm Violation → Perceived Control

b = −0.32, SE = 0.12, p = .010.

Participants exposed to the high-norm-violation condition reported lower perceived control.

Path B1 — Perceived Control → Privacy Concern

b = −0.31, SE = 0.06, p < .001.

Higher perceived control was associated with lower privacy concern.

Path B2 — Perceived Control → Willingness to Share Personal Data

b = 0.31, SE = 0.06, p < .001.

Higher perceived control was associated with greater willingness to share personal data.

The effects of norm violation on the two outcomes were established through ANOVA comparisons of condition means.

The mediation models separately estimated the direct effects after accounting for perceived control.

This pattern is consistent with partial, rather than full, mediation.

Perceived control represents one statistically supported pathway, but it does not explain the entire relationship between observed norm violation and AI aversion.

Other potential psychological processes, such as moral disapproval or perceived threats to human relationships, were not established as mediators in the main analyses.

Interpretation

Reduced perceived control helps explain why observing unconventional human–AI relationships is associated with stronger AI aversion.

However, because the mediator was measured rather than experimentally manipulated, the indirect effects should be interpreted as evidence consistent with the proposed mechanism rather than definitive evidence of causal mediation.

04 AI Autonomy: When Is the Indirect Pathway Stronger?

Study 3 examined whether the psychological response to observed norm violation depends on how autonomous the AI is perceived to be.

The experiment manipulated both norm violation and AI autonomy in a 2 × 2 design.

Step 1 — Examine Overall AI Aversion

Two-way ANOVAs showed significant main effects of norm violation on both privacy concern and willingness to share personal data.

However, the interactions between norm violation and AI autonomy were not significant for either primary outcome.

Interpretation

The findings support H3a and H3b.

When AI was framed as having low autonomy, observed norm-violating human–AI interaction reduced perceived control, and significant indirect effects emerged on both AI-aversion outcomes.

When AI was framed as highly autonomous, these indirect effects were not statistically significant.

Key finding: AI autonomy shaped the control-based psychological pathway, rather than significantly changing the overall effect of norm violation on privacy concern or willingness to share personal data.

This distinction matters for interpreting the findings accurately. Higher AI autonomy should not be treated as an experimentally validated solution that eliminates consumer aversion.

05 Additional Analyses: Validation and Alternative Explanations

The research also examined measurement validity and several additional possible explanations.

01 — Are Schema Congruity and Norm Violation Distinct?

Study 1 measured both schema congruity and perceived social norm violation.

A confirmatory factor analysis compared a two-factor measurement model with a one-factor alternative.

The two-factor model provided a significantly better fit:

Δχ²(1) = 262.43, p < .001.

The constructs were related but empirically distinct (r = −.49; HTMT = .52).

This supported treating expectations about AI's typical role and perceived social norm violation as related but separate concepts.

02 — Is the Effect Specific to Romantic AI Relationships?

A supplemental study (N = 287) compared four types of AI use:

  • AI romantic partner.
  • AI friend.
  • Conventional AI assistant.
  • AI assistant with anthropomorphic cues.

03 — Do Positive or Negative Emotions Moderate the Effects?

A supplemental experiment (N = 180 after exclusions) examined positive and negative affect as potential moderators.

The interaction effects were not statistically significant for privacy concern or willingness to share personal data.

These analyses did not provide evidence that the focal relationships varied according to the positive or negative affect measures tested.

04 — Does Perceived AI Literacy Change the Indirect Effect?

An additional analysis in Study 2 examined perceived AI literacy as a potential moderator of the indirect pathway through perceived control.

The indices of moderated mediation were not significant:

  • Privacy concern: IMM = −0.01, 95% CI [−0.0753, 0.0533].
  • Willingness to share personal data: IMM = +0.01, 95% CI [−0.0548, 0.0728].

The results did not provide evidence that the indirect effects varied with perceived AI literacy in this sample.

05 — Do Responses Differ Across Scenario-Gender Configurations?

A separate exploratory study (N = 91) compared responses across two scenario-gender configurations.

Neither privacy concern (p = .533) nor willingness to share personal data (p = .228) differed significantly.

Because participant gender and the gender of the scenario characters varied together, this analysis was not a formal test of gender as an independent moderator.

These additional analyses help clarify the interpretation and scope of the primary findings, without establishing that the effects are universal across all consumers or contexts.

06 Evidence Strength and Interpretation Boundaries

What the Research Supports

Consistent experimental effects

Three randomised experiments found that exposure to human–AI interaction high in norm violation increased privacy concern and reduced willingness to share personal data.

Evidence for a proposed psychological pathway

Study 2 identified significant indirect effects through AI-related perceived control.

A qualified role for AI autonomy

Study 3 found a significant interaction on perceived control and significant moderated mediation for both AI-aversion outcomes.

Measurement quality

Multi-item constructs demonstrated acceptable reliability and convergent validity in the reported confirmatory factor analyses.

What the Research Does Not Establish

Actual Data-Sharing Behaviour

The experiments measured self-reported willingness to share personal information rather than observed disclosure.

Definitive Causal Mediation

The mediation and moderated mediation analyses support the proposed explanation, but perceived control was not independently manipulated.

Elimination of AI Aversion Through Autonomy

AI autonomy did not significantly moderate the overall effect of norm violation on privacy concern or willingness to share personal data.

Universal Consumer Responses

The samples were recruited through Prolific and were predominantly drawn from Western, English-speaking populations.

Business or Financial Performance

The experiments did not measure customer acquisition, app retention, revenue, or the financial returns of specific marketing strategies.

Implications for Interpretation

These results demonstrate that publicly visible human–AI relationships can shape observers' privacy-related attitudes and intentions.

They do not establish that AI companions are objectively unsafe, that all intimate human–AI relationships are socially unacceptable, or that a particular product design will necessarily eliminate consumer resistance.

The findings provide a basis for developing and testing more informed AI product positioning and communication strategies.

Analysis Summary

Across three experiments, observing human–AI interaction that was high rather than low in norm violation produced consistently greater privacy concern and lower willingness to share personal data.

The analysis also identified a significant indirect pathway through AI-related perceived control, which was stronger when AI was framed as having low autonomy.

For AI companion companies, the evidence raises a broader consideration: how people are shown to interact with AI may influence how others evaluate the technology, even before they become users themselves.

The next stage translates these findings into implications for AI product positioning, privacy communication, and user-centred design.
