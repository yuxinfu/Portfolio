Report

AI Companionship & Consumer Aversion · Step 5 of 5

Insights & recommendations: understanding how socially visible human–AI relationships influence consumer privacy perceptions and willingness to engage with AI.

01 Executive Summary

As artificial intelligence evolves from a functional assistant into an emotional companion, companies are increasingly promoting AI products through the language of friendship, companionship, and intimacy.

While these experiences may appeal to their intended users, the way such relationships are perceived by others introduces an additional challenge for AI businesses.

This research examined whether consumers become more averse to AI after observing others engage in human–AI relationships that violate conventional social expectations.

Across three randomised experiments involving 973 participants, the findings show that observing romantic human–AI relationships, compared with conventional AI assistance, increased privacy concern and reduced willingness to share personal data with AI.

The research also identified AI-related perceived control as a significant psychological pathway, with the indirect effects emerging particularly when AI was framed as having low autonomy.

Decision Implication

Design for both AI users and the people who observe them.

AI companies should consider how their products are interpreted beyond direct users. Product positioning, communication of AI capabilities, and the visibility of privacy and control features may all influence broader consumer acceptance.

Direct User Experience

Create meaningful AI interactions

Preserve the emotional support, responsiveness, and functionality that target users value.

Public Perception

Make AI's role understandable

Communicate the nature, capabilities, and boundaries of AI in ways that help wider audiences understand what the technology does.

This report synthesises findings from an academic research project into potential managerial applications. It does not represent a commissioned consulting engagement, implemented product redesign, or observed commercial outcome.

02 What the Research Establishes

Four Findings and Their Business Implications

Key FindingSupporting EvidenceMeaning for AI Businesses
1. Observing AI intimacy can increase aversion.All three experiments found significantly higher privacy concern and lower willingness to share personal data following high- versus low-norm-violation exposure.AI acceptance is influenced not only by direct user experience but also by how AI use is socially observed.
2. Public-facing portrayals matter.Study 2: Privacy concern increased from 5.52 to 6.08, while willingness to share personal data decreased from 2.85 to 2.04 in the romantic-companion versus work-assistant advertising scenarios.Public representations of AI companionship deserve careful consumer research before launch.
3. Perceived control is an important psychological pathway.Study 2: Significant indirect effects through perceived control on privacy concern (+0.10) and data-sharing willingness (−0.10).Making users' control over AI understandable is a promising direction for further product and communication testing.
4. AI autonomy changes the indirect pathway.Study 3: Significant moderated mediation for both outcomes; indirect effects were significant under low AI autonomy but not high AI autonomy.Communication of AI autonomy may influence how consumers interpret emotionally intimate AI interactions, but does not necessarily eliminate aversion.

Keep the Strength of the Claim Aligned with the Evidence

The three experiments provide consistent evidence that exposure to norm-violating human–AI interactions can influence privacy-related attitudes and intentions.

However, several distinctions are important:

  • The studies measured self-reported willingness to share personal data, not actual disclosure.
  • Mediation analyses provide evidence consistent with the perceived-control mechanism, rather than definitive causal proof.
  • AI autonomy did not significantly moderate the overall effects of norm violation on privacy concern or willingness to share data.
  • The experiments contrasted scenarios involving different AI roles; they did not directly evaluate the effectiveness of specific advertising copy, privacy-interface changes, or product redesigns.

The recommendations below should therefore be understood as research-informed actions to evaluate, rather than experimentally validated interventions.

03 Recommendations for AI Companion Companies

01 — Evaluate Relational Positioning Beyond Direct Users

Directly motivated by Studies 1–3

Consider how AI companionship appears to observers

AI companion companies should examine not only how their products appeal to intended users, but also how the same experiences are interpreted by people who encounter them indirectly.

In the experiments, romantic AI relationships generated greater privacy-related aversion than conventional AI assistance.

Recommended action

Test audience responses to different, accurate portrayals of AI companionship, including descriptions that emphasise emotional support, responsiveness, and user-directed assistance.

Assess whether particular representations are perceived as exceeding AI's expected social role.

Why: Product positioning that appeals to one audience may generate concerns among another. Understanding both perspectives can help companies make more informed communication decisions.

02 — Make Privacy and User Control Visible

Managerial application informed by Study 2

Help consumers understand and manage their interactions with AI

The research identifies reduced AI-related perceived control as a significant pathway associated with privacy-related aversion.

This suggests that AI companion companies should consider how clearly their products communicate the boundaries of AI interactions.

Recommended action

Make relevant controls easy to locate and understand, including:

  • Personal data permissions and access settings.
  • AI memory and personalisation controls.
  • Interaction boundaries and user preferences.
  • Data deletion and account termination options.
  • Clear explanations of how personal information is collected and used.

These features should provide genuine functionality rather than merely reassuring language.

Why: Clear and meaningful user controls may help consumers understand what AI can access and how interactions can be managed.

The experiments did not directly test whether these interface features reduce AI aversion. Their effectiveness should be evaluated before implementation claims are made.

03 — Communicate AI Autonomy Accurately

Managerial application informed by Study 3

Balance AI capability with clear user oversight

Study 3 found that perceived AI autonomy shaped the indirect pathway linking observed norm violation to AI aversion through perceived control.

When AI was described as having low autonomy, the perceived-control pathway was stronger.

However, greater autonomy did not significantly reduce the overall effects of norm violation on privacy concern or willingness to share personal data.

Recommended action

Explain AI capabilities in accurate, accessible terms.

Clarify what the AI can do independently, when it follows user instructions, and how users can guide, restrict, or terminate interactions.

Avoid overstating AI autonomy or presenting systems as more humanlike than their actual capabilities justify.

Why: Accurate explanations of AI capabilities may help consumers develop clearer expectations about the technology.

The evidence supports investigating autonomy communication, not making AI appear more autonomous as a proven strategy for reducing aversion.

04 — Include Third-Party Observers in Consumer Research

Broader managerial application

Test how non-users respond to AI products

Conventional AI product research often prioritises adoption, satisfaction, and engagement among direct users.

However, the experiments demonstrate that people can form negative privacy-related responses simply by encountering information about how others use AI.

Recommended action

Extend concept testing and advertising evaluation to include people who have not used the product.

Measure privacy concern, perceived control, willingness to share personal data, and perceived appropriateness of the AI's social role.

Where relevant, examine responses across different audiences and communication contexts.

Why: Considering non-user audiences may reveal risks that are not visible in direct-user satisfaction or engagement metrics.

The following scorecard translates the research findings into proposed metrics for AI companion product and communication testing.

It is a proposed managerial application, not a dashboard deployed or evaluated in the experiments.

Recommended Measurement Approach

Primary research outcomes

For an initial communication experiment, predefine privacy concern and willingness to share personal data as primary outcomes.

Psychological diagnostics

Measure AI-related perceived control and social role expectations to help understand observed responses.

Behavioural validation

Where feasible and ethically appropriate, complement intentions with actual, voluntary permission decisions or product-use choices.

Product guardrails

Monitor direct-user satisfaction and experience alongside non-user responses, so that changes in communication are not judged from privacy measures alone.

Measurement Principles

Define outcome scales, comparison groups, observation periods, and success criteria before testing.

Do not assume that higher willingness to share personal data is automatically desirable. Appropriate, informed, and voluntary data sharing should remain a central requirement.

The research does not establish universal benchmarks, an optimal conversion target, or a monetary value for reducing AI aversion.

05 From Research to a Product Decision

A proposed four-stage implementation roadmap translates the experimental evidence into a practical research and development process.

01

Audit

Communication audit and priorities for testing.

02

Pretest

Shortlist of truthful communication alternatives.

03

Experiment

Comparative evidence, uncertainty, and trade-offs.

04

Decide

Proceed, refine, or gather more evidence.

Step 01 — Audit Existing AI Product Communication

Review how the company currently describes AI companionship across advertising, app-store listings, websites, and product onboarding.

Identify messages that emphasise emotional intimacy, friendship, romance, or the replacement of human relationships.

Assess whether those messages accurately represent the product's capabilities and whether controls and data practices are clearly explained.

Deliverable: A communication audit identifying claims and experiences requiring further consumer testing.

Step 02 — Develop and Pretest Communication Alternatives

Create truthful communication variants for the same AI product, varying how emotional support, user direction, and AI capabilities are emphasised.

Keep underlying product functionality consistent where the objective is to isolate the effects of communication.

Conduct initial concept testing with both prospective users and third-party observers.

Measure perceived norm violation, schema congruity, privacy concern, and perceived control.

Deliverable: A shortlist of communication approaches suitable for controlled evaluation.

Step 03 — Run a Controlled Consumer Experiment

Randomly assign participants to the communication variants, where feasible.

Predefine primary outcomes and plan the sample size around the effects the business needs to detect.

For an initial test, evaluate whether communication alternatives differ in privacy concern and willingness to share personal data.

Where appropriate, measure perceived control as an exploratory psychological pathway and assess whether findings vary across relevant audience groups.

If testing privacy-interface changes, isolate those changes from advertising message differences to clarify what causes any observed response.

Deliverable: Comparative evidence on consumer responses, uncertainty, and potential trade-offs.

Step 04 — Decide, Refine, and Monitor

Compare results across direct-user and third-party audiences.

Evaluate whether a proposed communication or design change reduces privacy-related concerns without misleading consumers or undermining valuable user experiences.

For promising variants, consider a limited real-world test with appropriate privacy safeguards and behavioural outcomes.

Continue monitoring consumer feedback and relevant product measures after implementation.

Deliverable: An evidence-based recommendation to proceed, refine, or conduct further research.

Illustrative Decision Rule

Advance a communication approach when it accurately represents the AI product, meets applicable privacy and safety requirements, and performs satisfactorily against predefined consumer and product criteria.

If the results are too uncertain, or if a message improves one outcome while creating material problems elsewhere, refine the approach and collect additional evidence.

This roadmap is a proposed future application of the research. No client implementation, budget, launch date, or realised commercial impact is implied.

06 Limitations and Further Questions

Interpretation Boundaries

1. Scenario-Based Experimental Evidence

All three main studies relied on hypothetical exposure scenarios rather than observing real-world AI companion use.

The findings support causal conclusions about the manipulated exposure scenarios and measured responses, but their implications for repeated exposure or live product experiences require further investigation.

2. Limited Forms of Norm Violation

The high-norm-violation conditions primarily involved romantic human–AI relationships.

Other unconventional uses of AI may generate different responses.

3. Attitudes and Intentions Rather Than Actual Behaviour

Privacy concern and willingness to share personal data were measured using self-reported scales.

The research did not directly observe personal data disclosure, permission decisions, app adoption, or discontinuation.

4. Limits of Causal Mediation

Perceived control was measured rather than independently manipulated.

The significant indirect effects are consistent with the proposed psychological mechanism but do not establish definitive causal mediation.

5. Cultural Generalisability

The samples were recruited through Prolific and were predominantly drawn from Western, English-speaking populations.

Social expectations about relationships and AI may differ across cultural contexts.

Questions for Further Research

Can greater visibility of privacy controls reduce AI aversion?

Future experiments could manipulate the availability and visibility of genuine user controls to determine whether they improve perceived control and reduce privacy-related concerns.

Do these effects translate into actual data-sharing decisions?

Behavioural experiments could examine permission settings, voluntary information disclosure, or decisions to engage with AI applications.

Do other types of AI relationships produce similar reactions?

Research could compare AI friendship, therapeutic companionship, romantic relationships, and other unconventional AI roles.

How do repeated exposure and real-world media affect perceptions?

Longitudinal or naturalistic research could investigate whether consumer responses persist, intensify, or diminish as AI companionship becomes more familiar.

Do cultural expectations change the effect?

Cross-cultural experiments could examine whether differences in perceived social norms influence responses to emotionally intimate AI use.

Related Exploratory Evidence

A supplemental experiment involving 287 participants examined schema congruity across romantic AI, AI friendship, conventional AI assistance, and anthropomorphic AI assistance. Romantic AI was significantly less congruent with expectations than the two instrumental conditions; the romantic-AI versus friendship contrast was not statistically significant. This supplemental evidence concerns schema congruity, not the full aversion pathway.

Other supplemental analyses found no significant moderation by positive or negative affect, and no significant differences across the two scenario-gender configurations examined.

These findings help define directions for further investigation, but do not establish that all forms of AI companionship or all consumer groups will respond identically.

07 Conclusion and Research Contribution

This project shifts attention from how consumers experience AI themselves to how they respond when they observe others interacting with AI.

Across three experiments, the research demonstrates that seeing others form romantic relationships with AI can increase privacy concern and reduce willingness to share personal data, relative to observing conventional AI assistance.

It also identifies AI-related perceived control as an important psychological pathway and shows how this indirect pathway depends on perceived AI autonomy.

Together, the findings highlight a broader dimension of AI product acceptance: the social visibility of AI use.

AI companies operate not only within individual user experiences but also within social environments where people observe, interpret, and evaluate how technology is used.

Closing Recommendation

Design for users. Communicate for observers. Build trust through meaningful control.

AI companies should consider both the experiences of people who use their products and the expectations of those who encounter these products from the outside.

By combining accurate product positioning, transparent communication, genuine user controls, and systematic consumer research, businesses can better understand the opportunities and potential unintended consequences of relational AI.
