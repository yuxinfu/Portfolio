Research Design

AI Companionship & Consumer Aversion · Step 3 of 5

From research questions to experimental evidence: examining how observing human–AI relationships influences consumer privacy concerns, perceived control, and willingness to share personal data.

01 Introduction to Research Design

This research employs a quantitative experimental design comprising three randomised online experiments with 973 participants.

The research investigates how observing human–AI interactions that are high, rather than low, in perceived social norm violation shapes consumers' responses to AI.

Rather than examining people who directly use AI companions, the studies adopt the perspective of third-party observers: individuals who encounter information about how others interact with AI.

Three experiments progressively address different aspects of the research problem:

  • Study 1 — Establishing the Effect: Does observing someone else's romantic relationship with AI increase privacy concern and reduce willingness to share personal data?
  • Study 2 — Understanding the Mechanism: Can these responses be partly explained by changes in AI-related perceived control?
  • Study 3 — Identifying the Boundary Condition: Does perceived AI autonomy influence the psychological pathway connecting observed norm violation to AI aversion?

Research Design Overview

The three studies use different exposure contexts to investigate whether consumers respond similarly to unconventional human–AI relationships across interpersonal communication, advertising, and news coverage.

02 Research Methodology

Quantitative Experimental Approach

The research uses scenario-based experiments in which participants are randomly assigned to different descriptions of human–AI interaction.

Random assignment allows comparisons between participants who encounter conventional AI use and participants who encounter more emotionally intimate uses of AI.

The research manipulates two principal factors:

Factor 1 — Observed Human–AI Interaction

  • Low norm violation: AI is used as a conventional functional assistant.
  • High norm violation: AI is involved in a romantic relationship with a human.

Factor 2 — Perceived AI Autonomy (Study 3)

  • Low autonomy: AI is described as following and responding to human instructions.
  • High autonomy: AI is described as capable of acting without human intervention or approval.

The research examines two dimensions of AI aversion:

Privacy Concern

The extent to which consumers express concerns about AI collecting or accessing their personal information.

Willingness to Share Personal Data

Consumers' self-reported willingness to provide AI with access to different categories of personal data.

Hypotheses and Corresponding Analyses

Each hypothesis is linked to a specific analysis rather than being tested through an unnecessarily complex statistical model.

03 Experimental Design

The three studies share a common conceptual manipulation but vary the context through which participants encounter human–AI interaction.

Study 1 — Interpersonal Exposure

Research objective

Examine whether observing a friend's relationship with AI influences privacy concern and willingness to share personal data.

Experimental design

A single-factor, two-level between-subjects experiment.

Participants were asked to imagine that a friend introduced them to an AI system. They were randomly assigned to one of two conditions.

Low Norm ViolationHigh Norm Violation
A friend uses AI as a work assistantA friend forms a romantic relationship with an AI
AI occupies a conventional functional roleAI occupies an emotionally intimate role
The friend introduces an AI assistantThe friend introduces an AI girlfriend

Experimental Stimuli

The scenarios were accompanied by images depicting a friend introducing the AI through a mobile phone.

The low-norm-violation condition showed an AI assistant, while the high-norm-violation condition showed an AI girlfriend.

These visual materials helped establish the different human–AI interactions participants were asked to evaluate.

Sample

  • Initially recruited: 202 participants.
  • Final analytical sample: 198 participants.
  • Recruitment platform: Prolific.

Key measurements

  • Privacy concern.
  • Willingness to share personal data.
  • Schema congruity.
  • Perceived social norm violation.

Design purpose

Study 1 establishes the initial relationship between exposure to norm-violating human–AI interaction and privacy-related AI aversion.

Two manipulation-check constructs were included to examine whether the scenarios were perceived as both different in their fit with AI's conventional role and different in their degree of social norm violation.

Study 2 — Advertising Exposure

Research objective

Replicate the effects examined in Study 1 in a public advertising context and investigate AI-related perceived control as a proposed psychological mechanism.

Experimental design

A single-factor, two-level between-subjects experiment.

Participants imagined encountering an advertisement for an AI application while travelling on the subway.

They were randomly assigned to one of two advertising scenarios.

Low Norm ViolationHigh Norm Violation
Advertisement for an AI work assistantAdvertisement for an AI romantic companion
Emphasises workplace efficiencyEmphasises companionship and reducing loneliness
Conventional functional positioningEmotionally intimate relational positioning

This manipulation moves beyond interpersonal exposure to examine how the public presentation of AI-enabled services may shape observers' responses.

Sample

  • Initially recruited: 400 participants.
  • Final analytical sample: 396 participants.
  • Recruitment platform: Prolific.

Key measurements

  • Privacy concern.
  • Willingness to share personal data.
  • AI-related perceived control.
  • Schema congruity.

The study also measured perceived AI literacy for additional exploratory analysis.

Proposed Psychological Mechanism

Observed human–AI interaction high in norm violation

↓

Reduced AI-related perceived control

↓

Greater privacy concern and reduced willingness to share personal data.

The proposed indirect relationships were examined using separate mediation models for the two outcomes.

Design purpose

Study 2 investigates whether observed norm violation is associated with lower perceived control and whether perceived control statistically accounts for part of the effect on AI aversion.

Study 3 — News Exposure and AI Autonomy

Research objective

Examine whether the indirect effect of observed norm violation on AI aversion through perceived control depends on how autonomous the AI is perceived to be.

Experimental design

A 2 × 2 between-subjects factorial experiment:

  • Factor 1: Human–AI interaction high versus low in norm violation.
  • Factor 2: AI autonomy high versus low.

Participants imagined reading a newspaper article about how people interact with AI.

The norm-violation manipulation contrasted a report about increasing AI use in the workplace with a report about people forming romantic relationships with AI.

The autonomy manipulation varied the description of AI's capacity to act independently.

Sample

  • Initially recruited: 400 participants.
  • Final analytical sample: 379 participants.
  • Recruitment platform: Prolific.

Key measurements

  • Privacy concern.
  • Willingness to share personal data.
  • AI-related perceived control.
  • Schema congruity.
  • Perceived AI autonomy.

Design purpose

Study 3 examines whether AI autonomy moderates the relationship between observed norm violation and AI-related perceived control, and whether this moderation extends to the indirect effects on privacy concern and willingness to share personal data.

The design distinguishes moderation of the proposed indirect pathway from moderation of the overall effect on the two primary outcomes.

04 Sampling Strategy

Participant Recruitment

All three main experiments recruited participants through Prolific, an online research participant platform.

Participants were randomly assigned to experimental conditions, and the analyses used the final samples remaining after exclusions.

The use of online participants enabled standardised scenario presentation and comparisons across experimental conditions.

Sampling Considerations

Random Assignment

Participants were randomly assigned to the experimental conditions within each study, supporting comparisons between different forms of exposure.

Sample Coverage

The samples were recruited online and were predominantly drawn from Western, English-speaking populations.

Analytical Samples

The reported final samples exclude participants removed before analysis. The research reports the initial and final sample sizes for each study.

Generalizability

The samples were not designed to provide nationally representative estimates of consumer attitudes. The studies instead focus on identifying responses to experimentally manipulated scenarios.

05 Questionnaire Design

Questionnaire Architecture

The experimental questionnaires were designed to capture participants' perceptions and intentions after exposure to an assigned human–AI interaction scenario.

The main measurement components were:

A. Experimental exposureEstablish the assigned human–AI interactionInterpersonal, advertising, or news scenario
B. AI aversionAssess responses to AIPrivacy concern; willingness to share personal data
C. Psychological mechanismExamine perceived controlAI-related perceived control (Studies 2–3)
D. Manipulation validationAssess whether the scenarios conveyed the intended differencesSchema congruity; norm violation (Study 1); autonomy (Study 3)

All principal constructs were assessed using seven-point response scales, and the order of items within each scale was randomised.

Questionnaire Preview — Actual Measurement Items

Section B1. Privacy Concern

Construct: Concern about personal information and privacy in relation to AI.

Four items were used:

  • "I am concerned that AI will gather too much information about me."
  • "I am concerned about my privacy in relation to the AI."
  • "It bothers me when the AI asks me to grant access to my personal information."
  • "I consider it a threat to privacy if I give out personal information to the AI."

The same four-item measure was employed across the three main studies.

Section B2. Willingness to Share Personal Data

Construct: Self-reported willingness to provide AI with access to personal information.

The measure covered four categories of access:

  • Access to your contacts.
  • Access your location.
  • Access to your albums.
  • Access to your web browser history.

Participants rated their willingness to share these categories of information with AI.

This measure captures intended data sharing rather than observed disclosure behaviour.

Section C1. AI-Related Perceived Control

Construct: The extent to which consumers feel able to manage and direct their interactions with AI.

Four items were used:

  • "I feel in control when using AI."
  • "I decide freely on how to use AI."
  • "I am in charge of the interactions happening through AI."
  • "I feel powerful when using AI."

This measure was introduced in Study 2 and retained in Study 3.

Section D1. Schema Congruity

Construct: The extent to which the observed interaction fits consumers' existing expectations about AI.

Three items were used:

  • "The AI in the scenario fits my expectations of what the role of an AI should be."
  • "The behaviour of the AI in the scenario was consistent with how I imagine such technologies operate."
  • "The AI in the scenario felt typical of its kind."

The measure was administered across all three main studies as a manipulation check.

Section D2. Perceived Norm Violation

Construct: Whether the observed interaction departs from accepted social expectations.

Three items were used in Study 1:

  • "This interaction violates social norms."
  • "This interaction is outside what people would normally consider acceptable."
  • "The way of interacting with AI is inappropriate."

Study 1 assessed both norm violation and schema congruity. Studies 2 and 3 retained schema congruity as the principal check of the norm-violation manipulation.

Section D3. Perceived AI Autonomy

Construct: Perceived capacity of AI to operate independently.

Study 3 included a single-item manipulation check:

"Do you perceive the AI described in the newspaper article as autonomous?"

Response scale: 1 = Low autonomy; 7 = High autonomy.

This item was used to assess whether participants perceived the intended difference between low- and high-autonomy AI descriptions.

Questionnaire Design Principles

1. Consistent Outcome Measures

The same primary dependent variables were measured across all three experiments, supporting comparisons across studies.

2. Random Assignment

Experimental conditions were randomly assigned to enable comparisons between different human–AI interaction scenarios.

3. Multi-Item Measurement

Privacy concern, willingness to share personal data, and AI-related perceived control were assessed using four-item scales.

4. Manipulation Validation

Checks were included to assess whether participants perceived the intended distinctions between human–AI interaction scenarios and AI autonomy descriptions.

5. Scale Reliability and Validity

Confirmatory factor analyses and reliability assessments were used to evaluate the multi-item measurement constructs.

Schema congruity was also measured in Study 1, but its Cronbach's alpha is not separately reported in the main Study 1 methodology. The thesis reports composite reliability and confirmatory factor analysis results for the construct.

All multi-item constructs demonstrated acceptable standardised factor loadings, average variance extracted (AVE) above .50, and composite reliability (CR) above .70.

Manipulation Validation

Study 1 — Norm Violation and Schema Congruity

Both perceived norm violation and schema congruity were measured to examine whether the scenarios conveyed the intended distinction.

An additional confirmatory factor analysis examined whether these constructs were empirically distinct.

Study 2 — Advertising Scenario

Schema congruity was used to assess whether the AI companion advertisement was perceived as less consistent with conventional expectations of AI than the work-assistant advertisement.

Study 3 — AI Autonomy

Two manipulation checks were used:

  • Schema congruity for the norm-violation manipulation.
  • Perceived AI autonomy for the autonomy manipulation.

Analytical Strategy

The research uses three complementary analytical approaches:

ANOVA

One-way and two-way ANOVAs compare experimental conditions on privacy concern, willingness to share personal data, and perceived control.

Mediation Analysis

PROCESS Model 4 examines indirect effects through AI-related perceived control in Study 2, with 5,000 bootstrap samples.

Moderated Mediation Analysis

PROCESS Model 7 examines whether AI autonomy moderates the proposed first-stage indirect pathway in Study 3, with 5,000 bootstrap samples.

Each analysis is aligned with the corresponding hypothesis.

Design Limitations

Scenario-Based Exposure

Participants responded to hypothetical human–AI interaction scenarios rather than observing actual AI companion use over time.

Operationalisation of Norm Violation

The high-norm-violation condition primarily involved romantic or emotionally intimate AI relationships. Other forms of human–AI interaction that violate social norms were not directly examined in the three main experiments.

Self-Reported Outcomes

The research measured privacy concern and willingness to share personal data, not actual data disclosure or subsequent adoption behaviour.

Interpretation of Mediation

Although the experimental conditions were randomised, AI-related perceived control was measured rather than independently manipulated.

The indirect effects therefore provide evidence consistent with the proposed psychological mechanism, not definitive proof of causal mediation.

Sampling Boundaries

Prolific-based samples, predominantly drawn from Western, English-speaking populations, limit the extent to which findings can be generalised across countries and cultural contexts.

07 Research Design Summary

The research design consists of three complementary randomised experiments examining how third-party observers respond to human–AI relationships.

Study 1 establishes the initial effects of observed norm-violating human–AI interaction on privacy-related AI aversion.

Study 2 replicates the investigation in an advertising context and examines AI-related perceived control as a proposed psychological mechanism.

Study 3 extends the research to a news exposure context and tests whether AI autonomy changes the indirect pathway through perceived control.

Together, the three studies provide a structured empirical basis for examining how socially visible AI use shapes consumer privacy perceptions and willingness to share personal information.

The design combines controlled experimental comparisons with psychological measurement and hypothesis-specific statistical analyses.
