Why Can Promoting AI Companions Backfire?

Three experiments reveal why promoting intimate human–AI relationships can heighten privacy concerns and reduce willingness to share personal data—even among consumers who have never used AI companions.

The Question

AI companies are increasingly designing products that go beyond functional assistance to offer emotional companionship. Applications now position AI as a friend, confidant, or even romantic partner.

While these services may appeal to people seeking companionship, they also introduce a broader challenge for businesses: the way some consumers interact with AI may influence how others perceive the technology.

A person does not need to use an AI companion to form an opinion about it. Simply encountering an advertisement, social media post, or news story about someone developing an intimate relationship with AI may change their willingness to engage with AI services more broadly.

The project therefore focused on three questions:

  • Does observing emotionally intimate human–AI interaction increase consumers' aversion to AI?
  • Why might witnessing someone else's relationship with AI increase privacy concerns and reduce willingness to share personal data?
  • Does the way AI autonomy is communicated change how observers respond to these interactions?

The Study

Design

A three-study experimental research programme examining how different portrayals of human–AI interactions influence third-party observers.

Participants encountered scenarios involving either conventional AI use, such as workplace assistance, or interactions involving romantic attachment to AI. The research examined whether these portrayals affected privacy concern, perceived control, and willingness to share personal data with AI.

Sample

Study 1 — Interpersonal exposure

198 participants evaluated a scenario in which a friend used AI either as a work assistant or as a romantic partner.

Study 2 — Advertising exposure

396 participants encountered a hypothetical advertisement for either a productivity-focused AI assistant or an AI companion offering romantic companionship.

Study 3 — News exposure & AI autonomy

379 participants viewed a hypothetical news story about conventional or romantic human–AI interaction. The experiment also varied whether the AI was described as having low or high autonomy.

Total: 973 participants across three randomised experiments.

Analysis

  • Randomised between-subjects experiments
  • One-way and two-way ANOVA
  • Confirmatory factor analysis
  • Mediation analysis
  • Moderated mediation
  • Bootstrapped confidence intervals

All three studies measured privacy concern and willingness to share personal data using seven-point scales. Studies 2 and 3 additionally examined AI-related perceived control.

What the Research Found

01 — Observing intimate human–AI relationships increased AI aversion

People became more privacy-conscious and less willing to share personal data after exposure to norm-violating human–AI interactions.

In Study 2, participants exposed to advertising for an AI romantic companion reported significantly greater privacy concern and lower willingness to share personal data than those exposed to advertising for a conventional AI work assistant.

This pattern was consistently observed across all three experiments, including scenarios involving interpersonal communication, advertisements, and news stories.

Key insight: Consumer aversion to AI can emerge through social observation, even without direct experience of the technology.

02 — The underlying mechanism was a reduced sense of control

The issue was not simply that people disliked unconventional AI relationships. Observing these relationships also made AI feel less controllable.

Study 2 investigated AI-related perceived control as a potential psychological mechanism.

Participants exposed to the AI companion advertisement reported a lower sense of control in relation to AI than those exposed to the conventional AI assistant advertisement.

Mediation analyses showed that lower AI-related perceived control was associated with greater privacy concern and reduced willingness to share personal data.

The indirect effects through perceived control were statistically significant for both outcomes.

  • Privacy concern: indirect effect = 0.10, 95% CI [0.0224, 0.1925].
  • Willingness to share personal data: indirect effect = −0.10, 95% CI [−0.1858, −0.0219].

These findings suggest that perceived control helps explain the relationship between observing norm-violating AI use and AI aversion, although it does not account for the entire effect.

Key insight: Consumers' responses to AI depend not only on what the technology can do, but also on whether its social role feels predictable and manageable.

03 — How AI autonomy is framed changes the psychological response

The same human–AI relationship can be interpreted differently depending on how autonomous the AI is perceived to be.

Study 3 tested whether perceived AI autonomy changes the psychological pathway linking observed human–AI relationships to consumer aversion.

When AI was described as having low autonomy, observing an intimate human–AI relationship significantly reduced perceived control.

When AI was described as highly autonomous, the difference in perceived control was not statistically significant.

Further analysis indicated that the indirect effects of observed norm violation on privacy concern and willingness to share data through perceived control were significant under low AI autonomy, but not under high AI autonomy.

Importantly, AI autonomy did not significantly change the overall effects of norm violation on privacy concern or willingness to share data.

Key insight: AI autonomy shapes the psychological mechanism behind consumer aversion, but higher autonomy does not necessarily eliminate negative consumer responses.

Recommendations

01 — Position AI companionship carefully

AI companies should consider how relational messaging is interpreted not only by their intended users but also by the wider public.

Messages presenting AI as supportive, responsive, and helpful may communicate a different social role from messages suggesting that AI replaces human intimacy or becomes a romantic partner.

Recommendation: Evaluate emotionally intimate product positioning alongside more assistive messaging, particularly in advertising, app-store descriptions, and social media campaigns.

02 — Make privacy and user control visible

The findings identify perceived control as an important psychological factor associated with privacy-related AI aversion.

For AI companion products, this suggests the value of making the boundaries of human–AI interaction clear and understandable.

Recommendation: Communicate how users can control their interactions, manage AI memory, set permissions, review data practices, and end interactions.

These are research-informed design recommendations rather than interventions directly tested in the experiments.

03 — Evaluate how non-users respond to AI products

Traditional product research often focuses on the experiences and satisfaction of existing or prospective users.

However, AI companion products may also affect people who encounter them indirectly through public advertising, news coverage, or social media.

Recommendation: Include third-party observers in concept testing and communication research. Measure privacy concern, perceived control, and willingness to share personal information alongside conventional adoption and engagement metrics.

The goal is to understand both the appeal of AI companionship to direct users and its potential consequences for broader consumer acceptance.
